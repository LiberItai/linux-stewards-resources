# Choosing and using an AI assistant for your small business

A plain-English guide to picking an everyday AI assistant — the kind you chat
with to draft, summarise, explain and plan — and using it without handing over
your customers' data. This is about the assistant on your desk, not automation
software that runs jobs for you (that is a different decision; see the
automation-readiness checklist in this repository).

Maintained by [Linux Stewards](https://www.linuxstewards.com), a UK-based
technology-services business.

## What an AI assistant is actually good at

- Summarising a long email thread, document or meeting note.
- Drafting a first version of an email, advert or job description.
- Explaining a technical term, an error message or a contract clause in plain words.
- Turning bullet points into a tidy table, list or short report.
- Suggesting ideas you then judge yourself.

It is *not* reliable for: facts you have not checked, legal or financial advice,
and anything where an error would cost you money or trust. Treat its output as a
first draft, not the final word.

## The three questions that matter most

1. **Where does the data go?** Free consumer assistants can use your input to
   improve the model. For business work, turn off chat history / training where
   available, or use a business tier that promises not to train on your data.
   Never paste customer names, payment details or personal data into a chat you
   have not verified is private.

2. **Which tasks will it actually do every week?** Pick one or two repeatable
   jobs (email drafting, note summaries) rather than buying a tool and hoping for
   a use later.

3. **What does it cost at the volume you need?** A per-user subscription is often
   cheaper than building something custom when only one or two people use it.

## Hosted vs private vs local

- **Hosted assistant** (a web or desktop app from a known provider): fastest to
  start, needs an account, sends your text to their servers. Fine for non-sensitive
  drafting once you check the privacy settings.
- **Business/enterprise tier**: adds data-handling commitments and admin controls.
  Worth it when several staff use it and you handle customer data.
- **Local model** (runs on your own computer, e.g. an open model): nothing leaves
  the machine, but it needs a reasonably capable computer, setup, and it is
  slower and less polished. The privacy trade-off is real.

The right choice depends on how sensitive the work is and who is using it — there
is no single "best" assistant.

## Writing prompts that work

A good prompt states the **role**, the **task**, the **input**, and the
**format**:

> Act as an editor for a small IT-support business. Turn the notes below into a
> polite three-sentence reply to a customer asking about price. Keep it under 60
> words and do not invent prices.

Compare that with "write a reply" — the more specific the instruction, the more
useful the answer, and the easier it is to spot when it is wrong.

## A simple safe-use checklist

- [ ] Check the privacy setting: is chat history / training turned off for business work?
- [ ] Do not paste customer personal data, passwords, keys or payment details.
- [ ] Verify any fact, figure, price or quote before you send it on.
- [ ] Review the wording — remove anything that sounds unlike your business.
- [ ] Keep a note of which tasks you actually use it for, so you can judge if it is paying off.
